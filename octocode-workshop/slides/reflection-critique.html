The pattern first. Reflection is one of the four agentic design patterns Andrew Ng lists (with tool use, planning and multi-agent): the model drafts, a critic reviews, the model revises, and the loop repeats. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) stores the agent's own notes on feedback and uses them on the next try: 91% pass@1 on HumanEval vs GPT-4's 80%. Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) has one model draft, critique and revise: about 20% better on average across 7 tasks. Anthropic calls it the evaluator-optimizer workflow and says it works best when the grading criteria are clear. The catch: Huang et al. (ICLR 2024) found models struggle to fix their own reasoning without outside feedback, and sometimes get worse. So the critique needs something real to look at: tool calls, logs, a fresh reader. That is how I use it in Octocode. Click 2, reflect on tool usage: at the end of a task, ask the agent how the tools went: which call was wasted, which hint it skipped, what it expected a tool to return. The answers point at the description, schema or hint to change. Click 3, interview fresh agents: start a new agent with no history and no context from the build session, give it a real task with the Octocode CLI, then interview it: why did you pick that tool, what did it return? Then compare its answers with its actual tool calls. Where its story doesn't match the log, the tool text is unclear. Click 4, strong builds, small tests: I plan and build with strong models (Opus, Sonnet) and test and reflect with small ones (Haiku and other small models). A small model with no context is the closest stand-in for the agent that meets your tool description cold. Anthropic's research system uses the same split: an Opus lead with Sonnet helpers beat Opus alone by 90.2% on their internal eval. This is my own practice, not a measured result; the measurement is the benchmark on the next slide.
